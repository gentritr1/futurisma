01

The idea, in one breath

Gate 01 · Beach · 0 m

FUTURISMA is a hover-racing game that looks like a PlayStation 2 game from 2002. Every map so far was industrial: airfields, salt works, a drowned reactor, a rocket pad. We wanted one map that was a dream instead.

Two short videos by an account called "saveroom" showed the look: a beach with plastic-looking palm trees and a very blue sea, and a night scene with glowing waterfalls, a grandfather clock in a jungle, and goldfish floating in the air. Cheerful, naive, impossible, and completely sure of itself.

The level idea came fast: you race three laps around an island. A clock tower stands in the middle. When it strikes, the sky turns to stars over twelve seconds, the waterfalls glow blue, and the fish rise out of the water and drift over the track for the rest of the race.

The one rule the game has

Every effect must matter for racing, not just look nice. So the night has a job: at night the water lights the edges of the road. You lose the far view but gain glowing rails. Braking points move. That sentence is the reason the whole feature was allowed to exist.

02

Who did what

Gate 01 · Beach · 150 m

This was not one AI doing everything. It was a small crew with strict jobs, and the jobs never crossed.

The person

Gentrit

Had the idea, made every decision that was a matter of taste, chose when to commit, and is the only one who can say whether it is fun.

The planner and judge

Claude (Fable)

Read the game's code, wrote the plans ("briefs"), decided the numbers, and checked every result by running it again — never by reading the report and nodding.

The builder

Claude (Opus)

Wrote the code for each phase from the brief, ran the tests, and reported back with proof. Was never allowed to commit.

The 3D artist

Codex ("Astra")

Modelled the towers, the cliff and the sea stacks in Blender, then did two polish rounds. Made its own reference pictures with GPT Image 2 when it needed them.

Pictures and sound

Higgsfield

Generated every reference image, texture sheet, sky and sound clip — 41 images and 8 audio clips for about 50 credits in total.

The back-and-forth worked like this: Fable writes a brief that a stranger could build from. Opus or Codex builds it and writes a report with numbers. Fable reruns the numbers, looks at the pictures, and says approve or change this. Only then does anything get committed. Two implementers even worked in the same folder at once — with a written list of which files belonged to whom.

03

Day one: is the look even possible?

Gate 02 · Grove · 300 m

Before writing any plan, we spent three credits to find out whether an image model could draw the look at all. It could — the first two "hero" frames looked like a real PS2 game. But a sheet of sixteen palm leaves came back as the same four leaves copied four times. That taught us the first rule of the picture pipeline: mood pictures go to one model, counted things go to another.

Then we parked it. The rocket-pad map was still being finished.

04

Reading the engine before writing the plan

Gate 02 · Grove · 500 m

A plan is only as good as what it knows about the code. So before the brief, ten separate agents each read one part of the game and reported back with file names and line numbers: how a map registers itself, how the road file is built, how scheduled events work, how the sky is drawn, what the budgets are, what the tests check.

They found five traps that would each have cost a day. The best one: adding a new map name silently makes the sky code crash on the very first frame, because a lookup returns nothing and the next line reads from it. Another: the game's own smoothing makes any light change take about one second — so a twelve-second sunset cannot be done the obvious way; the map has to blend it itself, every frame.

The brief came out at about 240 lines, with a route table, a schedule, budgets, and eight decisions that were flagged as Gentrit's to make. He handed all eight back and said: decide them yourself and start.

05

Phase A — a road you can drive

Gate 03 · Point · 680 m

Phase A made the island exist as a race, with no art at all: a 2,400-metre loop of 800 points, eight gates, seven named sections, a clock schedule, pickups, three rival skill levels, and grey boxes where the towers would go. Drag the handle to see how far it travelled.

after polishphase A⇔

The important habit started here: no number was typed by hand if it could be measured. The strike time is not "at 66 seconds" — it is "2.05 laps", where a lap is timed by actually driving the map with the autopilot, and the schedule file is generated from that recording. If the road changes, the recording is redone and every number moves with it.

Fable's check was not reading the code. It was running the same three-lap test and getting the same lap times to the millisecond: 32.433, 31.375, 31.400 seconds, zero missed gates.

06

Phase B — paint, sky, water, and real esses

Gate 03 · Point · 800 m

First the pictures. Six texture sheets — stone, metal, plants, water, signs, glowing things — each drawn as four squares in one image, plus two sky panoramas. Fable generated them, looked at each one, and wrote down what was wrong with it before handing them over.

Then Opus built the island in Blender from those sheets: 18 kinds of object, 533 of them placed around the track, 67,526 triangles. It also fixed the palm-grove corner, which had come out as one lazy curve instead of the wiggly "esses" the plan asked for — which meant re-timing the lap and regenerating the schedule again.

A bug no reading would have found

Opus built a tool that renders one object on its own and checks which square of the texture sheet it is really sampling. It found the road was drawing the kerb texture — a teal road — because two parts of the code disagreed about which way is up in an image. This exact mistake had shipped in this game twice before. The tool is now part of the test suite.

Both skies failed their quality test, and Opus said so instead of bending the test: the day sky's clouds were uneven, and the night sky was so dark that a "brightest divided by darkest" rule could never pass it. Fable regenerated the day sky (three tries; the one that passed untouched looked flat, so we kept the pretty one and softened its clouds by a measured 25%). The night-sky rule got a new clause for dark skies — with proof that the two older maps' skies scored exactly the same before and after.

07

Codex builds the towers — and our numbers were wrong

Gate 04 · Basin · 940 m

While Opus painted the world, Codex built four "hero" objects in higher detail: the clock tower, the watchtower with a tunnel through it, the waterfall cliff, and four sea stacks. It worked from front/side/top drawings that Higgsfield had produced for half a credit each.

The watchtower was not approved. Fable's plan said: a tower 16 metres wide with a 14-metre tunnel through it. Codex built exactly that — and it looked like a table on two legs, because a 14-metre hole in a 16-metre tower leaves one metre of wall each side. Nobody had checked the two numbers against each other. The plan was fixed (a 28-metre base, a real arch), Codex rebuilt it, and a rule was written down: when you describe a hole, say how much wall is left.

Then it turned out even the new numbers could not work: the road through the tunnel is on a curve, and a curved 14-metre road sweeps a 14.8-metre path. The tower now sits 10% larger than drawn, and a test checks the road fits through the hole.

08

Phase C — the night has to be measured before it can be tuned

Gate 04 · Basin · 1,100 m

Nothing in the game had ever measured a sunset. So before anyone touched a brightness value, Opus built a tool that freezes the camera on the beach and renders the same view at five points of the day-to-night blend, then measures the sky and the road in each. Only after that capture existed was a target written — as a difference from the measurement, not a number someone liked.

That same tool found two bugs Phase B had shipped: half the glowing reef edge was facing the wrong way and being culled, and the beach foam was drawn under the sea plane. Both were invisible in every earlier screenshot and obvious in a measurement.

Phase C also brought Codex's towers into the world, gave the goldfish paths that stay at least six metres above the road (measured two ways: 8.44 metres), and proved that the night mode does not get cheaper on shadows — the game keeps casting them even from a moon.

09

Why the night is fair

Gate 05 · Court · 1,400 m

Same beach, same camera, before and after the strike. Drag it.

nightday⇔

This is the trade. By day you see the whole island and brake by the landmarks. By night the fog closes in, but the water itself becomes your kerb. You are not being punished — you are being asked to read the road differently. A measured number backs it: the glowing edge is 0.41 brighter than the water beside it, and that contrast may only go up.

10

Polish, twice, with Codex

Gate 06 · Reef · 1,580 m

After the engineering was done, Fable wrote a list of nine things that were still wrong when you put the game beside the reference pictures — and for each one, a number that had to move. Codex worked through them: the sea got swell, the tunnel got lamps, the night road became readable between its glowing edges, the sunset stopped popping in its last quarter.

Round two was subtler — deeper sea colour, palms leaning over the road, stars that fade in only at the very end, glowing kerb stripes. Six of seven targets passed. The seventh, a beacon visible from 624 metres away through fog, did not — and that was Fable's fault, not Codex's: the target had been written without measuring first. It was withdrawn, and written down as a mistake.

Codex caught a contradiction in the plan

One rule said "a re-run must match within ±0.004". Another said "make the night road brighter". Read literally, the first forbids the second. Codex stopped and asked instead of guessing. The answer: the first rule tests repeatability of a build, not a freeze of old values. Asking was the right call.

11

Phase D — sprint, time attack, and a bug from before

Gate 07 · Reef · 1,800 m

A two-lap sprint would never reach a strike scheduled at 2.05 laps — the map would lose its night. So sprint got the same schedule squeezed to the same fraction of the race: the night lands a third of the way into lap two. Time attack got a ghost that records on your first run and races you on the second. Saves were measured against the game's 64-kilobyte limit: 17.6 KB used.

Building time attack exposed a bug from an older map: with no rivals, the starting-grid list still showed four placeholder cars. It was hidden because the test screenshots were too short to show that part of the screen. Fixed for every map at once, with a test that fails on the old code.

13

Words we used, explained simply

Gate 08 · Cut · 2,200 m
Brief
The plan. Written so that someone with no memory of the conversation could build from it alone.
Validator
A test that throws an error if a rule is broken. There are more than seventy; the map added six.
Soak
Letting the autopilot drive three laps in a hidden browser and recording everything: times, gates, frame time, draw calls.
Draw call
One "please draw this" message to the graphics card per frame. Fewer is faster. The budget is 145; the map uses 94.
Atlas
One image holding several textures. The map has six: stone, metal, plants, water, signs, glow.
Panorama
A very wide picture wrapped around the sky. Day and night are two panoramas mixed together.
Tick
The clock the game counts in — 120 per second. The strike is tick 8014.
Eyeball
A person looking at a picture. The only test that can say whether something looks right.
14

What is still to do

Finish line · Beach · 2,400 m

The island is silent. It has no sound at all yet — no surf, no chime, no strike. Eight signature sound clips are now generated and waiting: the quarter-chime, the three tolls of the strike, the waterfall, the fish rising. The clock's hands do not move yet. The pickups are still grey boxes; a drawing for their real hardware is done. And nobody has driven it with a controller in their hands.

That last one is the point. Everything above was checked by instruments. Whether it is fun is the one measurement only a person can take.
